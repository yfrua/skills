# Method Sources and Limits of Applicability

This file records where each judgement method in `references/system-scope-model.md` comes from. It is not read at runtime.

The theory is there to support those judgement actions, not to prove the skill is already effective; actual performance still needs behavioural validation.

- **Donald Schön, problem setting and reflective practice**: supports finding the breakpoint and making the judgement, and lets factual feedback test a new frame; it does not mean that rewording counts as progress. See *The Reflective Practitioner*, especially "The Structure of Reflection-in-Action". [MIT course readings on reflective practice](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/11-965-reflective-practice-an-approach-for-expanding-your-learning-frontiers-january-iap-2007/pages/readings/).
- **Ralph Keeney, value-focused thinking**: generate actions item by item from the confirmed goals and necessary conditions, then see how the conditions must fit together; generation and evaluation stay apart, which prevents picking only among the approaches that appear first. Siebert and Keeney's five studies found that eliciting candidates one objective at a time and then in pairs helps form good candidates more than adding thinking time alone; the subjects were human decision makers, so this does not directly prove the skill works on a model. Maps to step 3 of the main file and section 3 of `references/system-scope-model.md`; the means-ends relation (each means serves a goal, and dropping the means sends you back to the goal to find another means) is also used in step 2 of the main file (subtraction). It cannot infer unstated facts from the goal, and cannot guarantee finding a single best solution. [Value-focused thinking: Identifying decision opportunities and creating alternatives](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0377221796000045), [Creating More and Better Alternatives for Decisions Using Objectives](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/opre.2015.1411).
- **Gabrielle Adams et al., subtraction is easily overlooked**: eight human experiments found that without a prompt to consider removal, participants less often spotted beneficial subtractive changes. Supports actively checking, before the action plan is generated, whether existing means can go; the research does not decide which step should be dropped, and does not prove that a model given this prompt will necessarily improve. What to subtract is decided by the current goal and the means-ends relations, and covers the user's approach as well as processes the agent set up itself. [People systematically overlook subtractive changes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33828317/).
- **Ronald Howard, strategy generation table**: combine actions at different decision points into complete plans whose parts work together, so that scattered actions or the name of an option cannot pass as an executable path. Only the method of combining into a coherent plan is borrowed here; it does not require drawing a table at runtime, enumerating combinations or forcing several options, and the concrete combination must fit the facts, the permissions and the choices of the people involved. Maps to candidate generation in step 3 of the main file. [Decision Analysis: Practice and Promise](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mnsc.34.6.679?journalCode=mnsc).
- **Ronald Howard, decision sensitivity analysis**: check whether the applicable approach changes when an uncertain condition changes; maps to the check of conditions of use while forming a path in step 3 of the main file. What is borrowed is a qualitative comparison: it does not require a numerical model for every question, and does not require deleting down to one option first; if the path itself misses something, sensitivity analysis cannot find a new approach for it. [Proximal Decision Analysis](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.17.9.507).
- **Chris Argyris, double-loop learning**: folded into the premise check in section 1 of `references/system-scope-model.md`, checking the goals, rules and premises that govern the action, with no separate duplicate step. [Double Loop Learning in Organizations](https://hbr.org/1977/09/double-loop-learning-in-organizations).
- **Donella Meadows, system leverage points**: help identify where to intervene, in goals, rules, information flows and feedback; does not assume that a higher level suits the current task better. [Leverage Points](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/).
- **Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber, wicked problems**: the problem definition and the solution shape each other, and the current problem may be a symptom of another problem; used to test the definition, and does not judge every task to be a boundless complex problem. [Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01405730).
- **Herbert Simon, nearly decomposable systems**: when interactions are weak, local treatment can work, which supports keeping the original frame and stopping the expansion. [The Architecture of Complexity](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/15-440/assets/READINGS/simon-architecture-of-complexity-1962.pdf).
- **Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw, agenda setting**: what the media stresses repeatedly, and from what angle it tells it, affects what the audience considers important and how it understands it; used for the agenda check in content and account work, not as a reason for every piece of content to explain theory. [The Agenda-Setting Function of Mass Media](https://doi.org/10.1086/267990).
- **George Pólya, How to Solve It**: test a conjecture by checking special cases and extreme cases; supports the overall approach of the condition stress test, and does not mean every condition has to be pushed. [How to Solve It](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Solve_It).
- **Russell Ackoff, idealized design; Genrich Altshuller, the TRIZ ideal final result**: the first works backward from an ideal state, the second imagines delivering the function without consuming resources; they map to pushing resources to zero in the condition stress test, to step 2 of the main file (subtraction: without this approach, can its function still be delivered), and to design from zero in "A Few Angles Outside the Ladder" (leave the goal untouched, and assume you were starting over: how would you design it). This skill no longer requires pushing the goal to its strongest: "strongest" is undefined, the model can only guess, and the user's goal ends up rewritten in an ever more extreme direction. [Idealized Design: Creative Corporate Visioning](https://isfcolombia.uniandes.edu.co/images/2020-intersemestral/23_de_junio/ackoff1993.pdf).
- **Jason Pierce and Herman Aguinis, the too-much-of-a-good-thing effect**: a factor that helps in moderation turns harmful past some point; maps to checking in step 2 of the main file (subtraction) whether continuing an action backfires, with the conclusion written at the start of the answer as a reminder; the conclusion needs facts showing it has already been overdone, and does not mean every means has such a turning point. [The Too-Much-of-a-Good-Thing Effect in Management](https://homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2012/12/The-Too-Much-of-a-Good-Thing-Effect-in-Management.pdf).
- **Robert Lucas, the Lucas critique**: actors adjust their own behaviour to a new policy, so effects estimated from the old behaviour stop holding; maps to pushing other participants to full adaptation, and does not mean every party will adapt. [Econometric Policy Evaluation: A Critique](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/crcspp/v1y1976ip63-64.html).
- **Ian Mitroff, type III error**: solving the wrong problem precisely; supports checking before delivery whether the question being answered holds, and does not mean every question has to be doubted. [Type III error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_III_error).
- **Paul Watzlawick, John Weakland and Richard Fisch, Change**: first-order change adjusts inside the original frame, second-order change changes the frame itself; "more of the same" sustains the problem, and the solution itself can become the problem; maps to the test and the rewrite method for "the approach sustains the problem", and is consistent with double-loop learning. [Change: Principles of Problem Formation and Problem Resolution](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/9963373).
- **Jacques Hadamard, well-posed problems**: a solution exists, is unique, and depends continuously on the input; maps to the three checks of well-posedness, where "stable" means how sensitive the answer is to the input facts. [Well-posed problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-posed_problem).
- **Presupposition failure and complex questions (Strawson et al.)**: when a question presupposes a proposition that does not hold, every direct answer goes wrong; maps to the premise check. [Presupposition Verification for Question-Answering](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.00391).
- **Russell Ackoff, four ways of handling**: shelve it, meet it with a stopgap, solve it, or change the system or environment so the problem no longer appears (Ackoff calls this dissolve); maps to treatment strength in step 3 of the main file, and is a different dimension from "whether the question holds". To avoid clashing with step 1 of the main file, "dissolve the user's question", this skill groups dissolve under step 2 of the main file (subtraction) and does not use that name for it. A stopgap decides only how much cost to spend, and does not replace step 3's check of the conditions that decide the result. [The Art of Problem Solving](https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/finance-economics-law/the-art-of-problem-solving-978-0-471-85808-9).
- **Thomas Wedell-Wedellsborg, problem reframing**: set the problem first, look outside the frame, rethink the goal, then solve; supports the approach in step 1 of the main file. [Are You Solving the Right Problems?](https://hbr.org/2017/01/are-you-solving-the-right-problems).
- **Clayton Christensen, jobs to be done**: asking "what job is this for" finds the need behind the means; maps to the root goal and the goals of the people involved. [Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done).
- **NASA Systems Engineering Handbook**: supports tracing to parent requirements, interface checks and acceptance against the overall goal; used in the system scope path, and is not a reason for every problem to be moved up level by level. [NASA Systems Engineering Handbook](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/nasa_systems_engineering_handbook_0.pdf).
- **Barbara Minto, the Pyramid Principle**: lead with the conclusion; when several items share a part, lift the shared part to the level above and say it once, leaving only the parts that differ below. Maps to "Say each thing once" in the main file's "Writing style". See *The Pyramid Principle*.
- **Paul Grice, the maxim of quantity**: give the other person as much information as they need, and no more. Maps to "Each action states two things" (with the reason only where the user would not think of it), "Test sentence by sentence" and "Say each thing once" in the main file's "Writing style", and to writing a failure case only when it would change what the user does. See "Logic and Conversation"; [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Paul Grice](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/grice/).
- **William Strunk, omit needless words**: the principle itself gives no test, so this skill turns it into a checkable question: delete this sentence, would what the user does change. [The Elements of Style](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37134).
- These three study human writing and communication, and cannot prove that a model following them will get shorter; the effect is judged by behaviour tests. They replace the earlier rule of compressing the wording after the draft: in one backtest that rule showed no shortening (about 1125 → 1177 non-whitespace characters).
- **Jens Rasmussen, abstraction hierarchy**: read a system in layers by means-ends relation, from purpose, abstract function, general function and concrete function down to physical form; it was later used in power plants, healthcare, aviation and other fields; supports that the level ladder can apply across domains. [Work domain analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_domain_analysis).
- **Jens Rasmussen, risk management in a dynamic society (1997)**: accidents often happen at the joins between the levels of government, regulator, company, management and the front line, and adding rules at a single level rarely prevents them; maps to "problems often sit at the handover between levels" in the level ladder. [Risk management in a dynamic society](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/risk-management-in-a-dynamic-society-a-modelling-problem/).
- **David Parnas, criteria for decomposing systems into modules (1972)**: split modules by the design decisions likely to change, hiding the parts that change often inside the module; maps to "split by cause of change" at the rules and structure level. [On the criteria to be used in decomposing systems into modules](https://blog.acolyer.org/2016/09/05/on-the-criteria-to-be-used-in-decomposing-systems-into-modules/).
- **Stafford Beer, the Viable System Model**: split an organisation into five subsystems, daily operations, coordination, control, outward intelligence and policy, each covering a different decision scope and time scale; it adds the idea of splitting by decision scope at the structure level, and does not require fitting the five layers onto every system. [Viable System Model](https://ieeemilestones.ethw.org/w/images/7/73/Viable_system_Model.pdf).
- **RFC 3439 (including the section "Layering Considered Harmful")**: internet architecture experience shows that too many layers bring performance and complexity costs; maps to the boundary "split only when splitting brings a visible improvement". [RFC 3439](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc3439/).
- **Anthropic, Building effective agents**: distinguishes fixed workflows from agents; the common structures are a chained sequence, routing by type, parallel, an orchestrator plus workers, and generation separated from review, and it advises starting with the simplest option; maps to the executor levels in `landing-ai.md`. [Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents).
- **Genrich Altshuller, the TRIZ separation principles**: in a technical contradiction, where improving one requirement worsens another, satisfy the two requirements separately by time, space, condition or system level; maps to resolving the contradiction in "A Few Angles Outside the Ladder". [TRIZ Method](https://thinkinsights.net/consulting/triz-method).
- **Gary Klein, the premortem (2007)**: before execution, assume the project has already failed and have the participants write down the reasons, which exposes risks optimism had hidden; maps to reverse. [Pre-mortem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-mortem).
- **Stellan Ohlsson, representational change theory**: insight comes from changing the representation of the problem, mainly through constraint relaxation (dropping a limit you assume by default) and chunk decomposition (splitting something treated as one block), and also through re-encoding the problem; these map to subtraction, structural splitting and changing the representation. [Knoblich, Ohlsson et al., 1999](https://www.kognition.uni-koeln.de/literature/Knoblich_etal_1999.pdf).
- **Lee Bolman and Terrence Deal, the four frames**: look at the same organisational problem through the structural, human resource, political and symbolic frames, avoiding a single frame; it comes from organisational analysis, and this skill uses it only as an optional angle. [Four-Frame Model](https://www.businessballs.com/leadership-styles/four-frame-model-bolman-deal/).
